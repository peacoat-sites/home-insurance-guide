The typical U.S. homeowner pays about **$2,490 a year** ($208 a month) for home insurance in 2026, according to NerdWallet's May 2026 rate analysis. Where you live changes that number more than almost anything else. Oklahoma tops the list at $7,255 a year, about eight times Hawaii's $900. Search or sort the table to see where your state falls.

In this data, 24 states cost more than the national figure and 26 states plus Washington, D.C. cost less.

The top of the list is dominated by the central U.S., where hail, tornadoes and severe thunderstorms produce frequent roof and siding claims. Florida ($2,845) and Louisiana ($2,020) land near or below the national figure here, which surprises many readers. Other surveys rank those two states far higher. They use different sample homes, and some report averages rather than medians, which very expensive coastal ZIP codes can pull up. That's a good reason to compare your premium against one source consistently rather than mixing numbers from different tables.

## What homeowners actually pay: the regulators' numbers

The table above shows quoted rates for one standardized home. State insurance regulators collect a different measure: total premium divided by the number of policies in force, which covers every kind of home and coverage amount. A report from the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' research center, published July 31, 2026, found:

- In 2024, 715 homeowners insurers wrote 103.3 million policies and $165.2 billion in premium, an **average of $1,600 per policy**.
- By region, the Southeast had the highest average at $1,818 per policy, followed by the West ($1,600) and the Midwest ($1,476). The Northeast was lowest at $1,396.
- From 2018 to 2024, the average premium per policy rose between 44% (Northeast) and 74% (West). After adjusting for inflation, the increase was 18.3% to 43.3%, with the biggest jumps in 2023 and 2024.
- Measured against home values, coverage got slightly cheaper overall: premium per $1,000 of home value was 2.2% lower in 2024 than in 2018, because median home values rose 56% over the same period. The West was the exception, 4.9% above its 2018 level.

The regulators' average is lower than the quoted figure partly because it includes smaller homes, lower coverage limits and older policies, so it isn't directly comparable to the state table. Both point the same way: premiums rose fastest in 2023 and 2024, and much faster in some regions than others.

## What moves your own premium

NerdWallet's 2026 analysis changed one factor at a time on its sample policy. The results show how much each lever matters:

| Change to the sample policy | Average annual cost | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline: $400,000 dwelling coverage, good credit, no claims, home built 1984 | $2,490 | |
| One prior claim | $2,750 | +10% |
| Poor credit | $4,290 | +72% |
| Newly built home (2025) | $1,425 | -43% |
| $300,000 dwelling coverage | $1,975 | -21% |
| $600,000 dwelling coverage | $3,510 | +41% |

A few practical takeaways:

- **Credit matters a lot in most states.** In a few states, including California, Maryland and Massachusetts, insurers can't use credit-based scores to price home insurance.
- **Small claims can cost more than they pay out.** One claim raised the sample premium by about $260 a year. For minor damage close to your deductible, paying out of pocket is often cheaper over time.
- **Coverage should match rebuild cost, not market value.** Dwelling coverage drives the price, so set it to what it would cost to rebuild. Our [replacement cost calculator](/home-replacement-cost-calculator/) gives a starting estimate.
- **Your deductible is a lever you control.** A higher deductible lowers the premium; just keep that amount in savings. Try our [deductible calculator](/home-insurance-deductible-calculator/) to see the break-even point.
- **Roof age and condition** have become major pricing and eligibility factors. Keep records of roof replacements and repairs to show insurers.
- **Bundling** home and auto with one insurer usually earns a discount, so ask for both the bundled and unbundled price.

## How to use this table

Find your state and compare it with your own premium, keeping in mind the sample home has $400,000 of dwelling coverage. If your coverage is much higher or lower, adjust your expectations using the coverage rows above. If you're paying well above your state's figure for similar coverage, get quotes from at least three insurers; that is the most reliable way to find out whether you're overpaying. If you're below it, you're likely in good shape, but re-check every year or two because rates in many states are still moving.
