Most people shopping for homeowner's insurance don't think twice about listing their dog on the application. Big mistake. I spent 14 years reviewing claims at a national insurer, and [dog bite](/homeowners-insurance-dog-bite-coverage/) liability was one of the most consistent sources of denied coverage, policy cancellations, and very awkward conversations with policyholders who had no idea this was even a factor.

Here's what actually happens: you buy a policy, you sign the application, somewhere on page four there's a question about "potentially dangerous animals," and you either miss it, misread it, or assume your well-behaved Rottweiler doesn't count. Then your dog nips a delivery driver, and you learn very quickly that your insurer knew something you didn't.

The breed question in homeowner's insurance is genuinely complicated, and I'll be honest, the industry's approach to it is inconsistent enough that even I've been surprised by which dogs trigger exclusions at which companies.

## Why Insurers Care (And Why the Number Surprises People)

Dog bite claims account for roughly a third of all homeowner's liability payouts in a given year. The Insurance Information Institute puts the average cost per dog bite claim at over $58,000 as of recent data, up significantly from prior years as medical costs and legal settlements have climbed. That's not a rounding error. That's a number that makes underwriters sit up straight.

What surprised me, even after years in claims, was how unevenly the financial risk distributes. A small number of incidents drive enormous payouts. A single severe bite on a child can result in reconstructive surgery, years of psychological treatment, and a lawsuit that settles well above six figures. Insurers aren't being irrational when they restrict certain breeds. They're reading actuarial tables.

That said, the execution is often sloppy and sometimes genuinely unfair to responsible owners.

A few things that table can't show you: "mixed breed" is a minefield. If your dog is visually identified as a pit bull mix by an adjuster after a claim, some insurers will treat it as one. I've seen this happen. The classification often comes down to a photo and an adjuster's subjective read.

Worked example: A reader in suburban Atlanta had a Cane Corso that her insurer had never asked about specifically. She'd listed "large mixed breed" on her application. Dog bit a neighbor's kid at a birthday party. Adjuster came out, took one look, pulled the breed exclusion clause, and denied the $47,000 liability claim. She had no coverage she thought she had. Her honest description might have flagged the issue upfront, or it might not, but the vague answer cost her the chance to find out.

## What "Excluded" Actually Means for Your Policy

This is where I want to slow down, because the language here matters and most people read past it.

An exclusion for a specific dog breed typically means your [liability coverage](/home-insurance-liability-coverage/) won't apply to any incident involving that animal. Your dwelling coverage, your personal property protection, your loss-of-use coverage, none of that is affected. But the liability piece, which is the part that pays if your dog injures someone on or off your property, is gone.

Some policies go further and cancel entirely if they discover a restricted breed after the policy is issued. This is called a "material misrepresentation" cancellation, and it's completely legal if the application specifically asked about your dog and you answered inaccurately.

The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC)](https://www.naic.org/) has noted that breed-specific underwriting practices vary significantly by state, and a handful of states (Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Nevada among them) have enacted statutes limiting an insurer's ability to deny or cancel based solely on breed. If you live in one of those states, you may have more protection than you think. The operative word is "solely," and insurers are creative about finding other reasons.

Worked example: A friend in Detroit with a registered Rottweiler called his insurer after reading something like this article. The agent initially flagged the breed. When he pointed to Michigan's restrictions on breed-only exclusions and pushed to speak with an underwriter directly, the policy was issued with a $500 annual surcharge instead of a denial. He wouldn't have known to push back without understanding the state rule first.

## Finding Coverage When You Own a Restricted Breed

I'm not going to pretend this is easy. But it's not hopeless either.

First, be completely honest on your application. I know that feels counterintuitive when you're afraid of the answer, but concealment is what turns a manageable problem into a policy cancellation after a claim. You want to know where you stand before the incident, not after.

Second, call multiple insurers. The breed lists are not uniform. An insurer that won't touch your dog might be right next door to one that will, at a modest surcharge. Independent insurance agents who work with multiple carriers are genuinely useful here because they can shop your specific situation without you having to call a dozen companies yourself.

Third, ask specifically about umbrella policies. Some homeowners get standard liability from one carrier (with a breed exclusion) and purchase a separate personal liability umbrella from a specialty insurer that doesn't restrict breeds. This isn't a guaranteed path and takes real underwriter buy-in, but I've seen it work.

Fourth, look at what behavioral documentation can do. Canine Good Citizen certification from the American Kennel Club, obedience training records, a trainer's letter, proof of professional socialization work. None of this guarantees coverage, but some underwriters will treat it as a mitigating factor. The [IBHS home fortification guides](https://ibhs.org/) also cover property security measures that can sometimes factor into broader risk assessments for difficult-to-underwrite properties. It's a marginal tool, but worth knowing about.

One practical thing that helps your position in any claim: maintain solid documentation of your home and belongings, including records of your dog's training and vet history. A home inventory app like Encircle (free on iOS and Android) can store photos and records in one place.
