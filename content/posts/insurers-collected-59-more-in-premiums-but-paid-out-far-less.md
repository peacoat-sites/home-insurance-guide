A bombshell report dropped today, November 19, 2026, and if you've been grinding your teeth over your homeowner's insurance bill for the past few years, you're going to want to read it closely. Americans for Financial Reform, Public Citizen, and the Revolving Door Project released "Home Insurers Are Ripping People Off Amid the Climate Crisis," and the numbers inside it are hard to look away from. [Home insurance premium](/how-to-lower-homeowners-insurance-cost/) revenue climbed 59% between 2021 and 2025, jumping from $114 billion to $181 billion. Claims payouts? Those rose just 28%. The industry spent years telling regulators, state lawmakers, and policyholders that rates had to go up because climate-driven losses were spiraling out of control. The data in this report tells a very different story.

The report calculates that insurers pocketed roughly $113 billion in excess underwriting profit over that four-year stretch. That's not investment income, not overhead, not reserves against future catastrophes. That's the gap between what came in through your front door and what went out to pay claims. In 2021, insurers paid about 69 cents in claims for every premium dollar collected. By 2025, that figure had dropped to 55 cents. The historical baseline from the 1990s sat between 74 and 80 cents. So insurers aren't just outperforming their worst years. They're operating in territory that has no real precedent in modern American home insurance.

The first half of 2026 made it worse. The [loss ratio](/home-insurance-loss-ratio/) fell further, to just 48 cents per premium dollar. Insurers were keeping more than half of everything homeowners paid. That's the number that shifts this from a bad trend to an active policy question.

## The State-Level Numbers Are Even Harder to Explain

The national averages are striking. The state-level breakdowns are what should be generating emergency legislative hearings. Texas and Florida, two states where insurers have lobbied most aggressively for rate increases by citing catastrophic weather exposure, show the sharpest divergence between premiums collected and money paid out.

| State | Premium Increase (2021–2025) | Claims Payout Change (2021–2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | +78% | -23% |
| Florida | +64% (to $18.7B) | -67% |
| National | +59% | +28% |

Florida's numbers deserve a second look. Premiums in the state reached $18.7 billion in 2025 while payouts fell 67%. Insurers spent years pointing to Florida as the cautionary tale, the place where litigation abuse and hurricane exposure made the market functionally uninsurable. Carriers exited. Rates exploded. Citizens Property Insurance, the state-backed insurer of last resort, swelled to over 1.4 million policies. The standard industry narrative was that Florida was hemorrhaging money. The report's data suggests the opposite was true, at least in the aggregate, by 2025.

Texas follows a similar arc. Premiums up 78%. Payouts actually down by nearly a quarter. The state has seen significant hail and storm activity in recent years, so the direction of that claims number is worth scrutinizing carefully. But even accounting for year-to-year volatility in weather events, a 78% premium increase alongside a 23% drop in payouts is not what a market in crisis looks like from the outside.

## The 30% Zero-Payment Rate Is the Number Most Policyholders Don't Know

One figure in the report cuts closer to home than the aggregate profit numbers. From 2021 through 2025, roughly 30% of homeowner claims were closed without any payment to the policyholder. No settlement. No check. Zero.

That's not a small rounding error. When three out of ten people who filed a claim got nothing, one of a few things is happening. Either a substantial portion of claims were genuinely outside the scope of coverage, or adjusters were denying valid claims at elevated rates, or policies had been quietly rewritten with exclusions and sublimits that most homeowners didn't notice when they renewed. Probably some combination of all three.

This is where the gap between marketing language and actual policy language does real damage. Carriers advertise "complete protection" and "peace of mind," but actual policies frequently exclude flood damage, earth movement, mold that develops after a covered water loss, and losses below increasingly high deductibles. The deductible structure alone, particularly hurricane and wind deductibles calculated as a percentage of insured value rather than a flat dollar amount, can turn what looks like coverage into a situation where a homeowner with a $400,000 policy owes the first $8,000 to $20,000 out of pocket before the policy contributes anything.

## What This Means Before Your Next Renewal

The report's release comes during a period when regulators in several states are already under pressure to approve additional rate increases for 2027. The findings from Americans for Financial Reform and Public Citizen give state insurance commissioners something concrete to push back against. That doesn't mean rates will drop. Insurance markets move slowly and state regulation is uneven. But the political and evidentiary ground has shifted.

For anyone renewing a policy in the next 90 days, a few practical things are worth checking. Ask your insurer or agent what your current wind or hurricane deductible actually is in dollar terms, not percentage terms. Ask whether your policy pays replacement cost value or actual cash value on personal property. Ask specifically whether water backup and sewer overflow coverage is included or excluded. These aren't exotic edge cases. They're the most common places where people discover, after a loss, that the coverage they thought they had was much thinner than the premium suggested.

If you've had a claim closed without payment in the past few years, the report's findings give you reason to look more closely at that denial. State insurance departments have consumer complaint divisions. Many state laws require insurers to provide a written explanation of any denial. That explanation is worth requesting and reading carefully, because a denial letter that cites a vague policy exclusion is often the starting point for a successful appeal, not the end of the road.

Consulting with a licensed public adjuster or an insurance attorney before accepting a denial is worth considering for any significant loss. This article isn't personal legal or financial advice, and your specific situation depends on your policy language, your state's laws, and the facts of your claim.

## The Broader Policy Question

The report explicitly calls for greater regulatory oversight, including public rate filings with supporting loss data, more aggressive review of rate increase requests, and stronger consumer protections around claim denials. Those are policy recommendations, and reasonable people can debate the right mechanisms. What's harder to debate is the underlying data.

Premiums went up 59%. Payouts went up 28%. Loss ratios fell from 69 cents to 55 cents to 48 cents. And 30% of claims were closed with nothing paid. That sequence of numbers, if it holds up to scrutiny, represents a significant transfer of wealth from homeowners to insurers during a period when many of those same homeowners were told the increases were unavoidable.

The industry will respond, and their response will likely center on investment losses, reserve requirements, reinsurance costs, and the long tail of catastrophic exposure. Those are legitimate factors in pricing. But the burden of proof has shifted today. The numbers in this report are specific, and the questions they raise deserve equally specific answers.
