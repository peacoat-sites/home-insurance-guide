An insurer's AM Best rating is one of the fastest ways to gauge whether that company will actually be standing when you need to file a claim. That's the whole point of the rating. It's not a customer service score or a price comparison tool. It measures financial strength: can this insurer pay what it owes?

AM Best has been rating [insurance companies](/best-homeowners-insurance-companies/) since 1906, which makes it the oldest credit rating agency focused specifically on the insurance industry. When you're evaluating a carrier, the AM Best Financial Strength Rating (FSR) is the number you want. Other ratings exist, including AM Best's Issuer Credit Rating and Long-Term Issue Credit Rating, but the FSR is what most consumers need to look at first.

## What the Scale Actually Means

AM Best uses letter grades with plus and minus modifiers, similar to academic grades but with its own specific meanings. Here's the full FSR scale:

| Rating | Category | What It Means in Plain Terms |
|--------|----------|------------------------------|
| A++ | Superior | Highest financial strength; minimal vulnerability |
| A+ | Superior | Excellent strength; very low vulnerability |
| A | Excellent | Strong ability to meet obligations; low vulnerability |
| A- | Excellent | Same category, slightly more exposure to adverse conditions |
| B++ | Good | Good ability to meet obligations; moderate vulnerability |
| B+ | Good | Same tier; somewhat more exposure |
| B | Fair | Fairly able to meet obligations; vulnerability to adverse conditions |
| B- | Fair | Same tier; more pronounced vulnerability |
| C++ | Marginal | Marginal ability; vulnerable to adverse conditions |
| C+ | Marginal | Same; more vulnerable |
| C | Weak | Weak ability to meet obligations |
| C- | Weak | Same; more severe |
| D | Poor | In or near default or liquidation |
| E | Under Regulatory Action | State regulators have intervened |
| F | In Liquidation | Company is being liquidated |
| S | Suspended | Rating temporarily suspended |

The practical cut-off for most buyers: stick with carriers rated B+ or higher. A through A++ is the comfort zone. That said, regional insurers sometimes carry B+ ratings and have solid long track records, so don't dismiss them automatically.

One thing the marketing brochures won't tell you: insurers can pay AM Best for a rating, or choose to remain "not rated" (NR). An NR designation doesn't mean the company failed review. It may simply mean they didn't participate. But you can't distinguish a deliberately unrated healthy insurer from one that declined to participate because the result would be unflattering. That ambiguity is a real gap.

Go to [ambest.com](https://www.ambest.com) and use the free consumer lookup. You don't need an account. Search by company name. What you'll see:

1. The current FSR letter grade
2. The rating outlook (Stable, Positive, or Negative) -- this matters more than people realize
3. The date the rating was last affirmed or changed

The outlook is the signal most readers skip over. A carrier rated A with a Negative outlook is worth watching. It means AM Best's analysts see conditions that could push that rating down. You may want to revisit in 6 to 12 months if you're on a multi-year policy or considering a long-term relationship.

**Example:** Assume you're comparing two homeowner insurers. Carrier X holds an A (Excellent) rating with a Stable outlook. Carrier Y holds an A- (Excellent) rating with a Positive outlook. All else being equal, Carrier Y's trajectory may actually be more favorable, even though today's raw letter grade is slightly lower. A Positive outlook typically signals AM Best expects the rating to improve. This distinction gets lost when people focus only on the letter.

## What AM Best Ratings Don't Tell You

This is where I want to push back against over-reliance on the rating. A high FSR tells you the insurer is financially sound. It tells you nothing about:

- Whether claims adjusters interpret policy language in your favor
- Average claim settlement timelines
- How aggressively the company contests losses
- How your specific policy is written, including exclusions

A carrier can be rated A++ and still deny a legitimate claim on technicical grounds buried in the exclusions section of your policy. Financial strength and claims behavior are separate questions. For claims behavior, your state's insurance department complaint ratios are a better data source. The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC)](https://www.naic.org/) publishes a Consumer Insurance Search tool that shows complaint index scores by company, which lets you compare how often a carrier draws complaints relative to its market share.

## Ratings in Context: Regional and Specialty Carriers

Large national carriers almost universally maintain A or above ratings. The question gets more complicated with smaller regional insurers, surplus lines carriers, and newer entrants to high-risk markets like coastal flood-prone areas or wildfire zones.

In some markets, particularly in states where major carriers have pulled back, your realistic options may include carriers with B++ or B+ ratings. That's not automatically a dealbreaker. Check whether the carrier is admitted (licensed by your state's department of insurance) or non-admitted (surplus lines). Admitted carriers are backed by your state's guaranty fund if the insurer becomes insolvent. Non-admitted carriers typically are not, which raises the stakes when the FSR is already lower.

Your state's insurance department (find yours through the [NAIC's state map](https://www.naic.org/state_web_map.htm)) can confirm admitted status and show any regulatory actions against a carrier.
