Most home insurance policies do cover appliances, but only under very specific circumstances. The coverage usually isn't what you'd hope for when your refrigerator stops cooling or your dishwasher springs a leak, and understanding exactly where the line falls could save you from a nasty surprise after a claim.

Your homeowner's policy will generally pay for appliance damage caused by a covered peril: fire, lightning, theft, vandalism, or certain types of sudden [water damage](/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/). What it won't cover is the vast majority of real-world appliance failures, which tend to be mechanical breakdowns, wear and tear, or manufacturer defects. Those aren't insurance events. They're maintenance problems, and insurers treat them differently.

## What "covered peril" actually means for your appliances

Think of your policy as covering disaster, not dysfunction. If a kitchen fire destroys your stove, your policy's dwelling or [personal property coverage](/homeowners-insurance-personal-property-coverage/) applies depending on whether the appliance is built-in or freestanding. Built-in appliances (wall ovens, built-in microwaves, dishwashers that are part of the cabinetry) often fall under dwelling coverage. Freestanding appliances (refrigerators, washing machines, dryers) are typically personal property.

The distinction matters because your personal property coverage usually carries a deductible, and there's a coverage limit that applies to the total of all your belongings. If your deductible is $1,500 and a fire damages your $900 stand-alone freezer, you're not filing a claim. The math doesn't work.

Covered perils under a standard HO-3 policy include fire, lightning, windstorm, hail, explosion, riot, aircraft, vehicles, smoke, vandalism, theft, falling objects, weight of ice or snow, sudden and accidental overflow of water from plumbing, and power surges from utility company problems. That last one trips people up. A lightning strike that sends a surge through your home and fries your appliances? Generally covered. A random power surge from grid issues? Usually covered. Your dryer's motor burning out after seven years? Not a covered peril. Never was.

## The water damage question

This is where most appliance-related claims actually happen, and it's the most confusing area of coverage. The rule of thumb: sudden and accidental water damage is usually covered. Gradual leaks are almost always excluded.

**Example:** A washing machine supply hose bursts while the washer is running. Water floods the laundry room and damages the flooring and drywall. Under a standard HO-3 policy, the resulting water damage (flooring, wall, etc.) would likely be covered, though the washer itself may not be covered for the mechanical failure that caused the burst. Assumptions: the homeowner has a standard HO-3 policy with dwelling coverage, the damage was sudden and not from long-term seepage, and the insurer's adjuster confirms the cause of loss.

If that same hose had been slowly dripping behind the washer for months and you hadn't noticed until you pulled it out, the resulting damage almost certainly wouldn't be covered. Gradual damage exclusions are real and enforceable.

## Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost

| Policy Type | How Appliance Payout is Calculated | Example: 8-Year-Old Fridge Bought at $1,200 |
|---|---|---|
| Actual Cash Value (ACV) | Replacement cost minus depreciation | May pay $200-$400 depending on depreciation schedule |
| Replacement Cost Value (RCV) | Cost to buy equivalent new item today | May pay $900-$1,200+ for a comparable model |
| Extended Replacement Cost | RCV plus a buffer (often 20-25%) | Useful for rebuilds, less relevant for appliances |

Most standard policies default to ACV for personal property unless you've specifically added replacement cost coverage. The difference on a 10-year-old appliance can be dramatic. An ACV payout on an older model might not cover even a fraction of what a new comparable unit costs.

If you have an HO-3 policy with ACV personal property coverage, ask your insurer what it would cost to upgrade to replacement cost. The annual premium difference is often modest, and the payout difference after a major loss is substantial.

## What home warranties cover (and what they don't)

A home warranty plan is a service contract, not insurance. It's designed for the breakdown scenario your homeowner's policy explicitly excludes: the refrigerator compressor that fails at year six, the HVAC unit that stops working in July. Companies like American Home Shield or Choice Home Warranty sell these contracts separately, typically running a few hundred dollars per year plus a service fee per claim.

They're worth considering, but read the exclusions carefully. Pre-existing conditions are often excluded. Coverage caps per appliance category are common. Some contracts won't cover items that weren't properly maintained. The [Insurance Information Institute (III)](https://www.iii.org/) has a useful breakdown of how home warranties differ from homeowner's insurance if you want to compare the two side by side before committing.

## Document your appliances now, not after a loss

A home inventory is the single most practical thing you can do to protect yourself before you need to file. Record model numbers, purchase dates, serial numbers, and approximate values. Store that document somewhere that isn't your house. Apps like the [National Association of Insurance Commissioners' myHOME app](https://content.naic.org/consumer/myhome-scr.htm) let you do this on your phone with photos.

Without documentation, appliance claims take longer, pay less, and get disputed more. Adjusters aren't unreasonable, but they can't pay for items they can't verify existed. A document safe (available on Amazon) is a reasonable place to store physical receipts and warranty cards. (Commission disclosure applies.)

Your state's insurance department is also a resource people overlook. The [NAIC's state department directory](https://www.naic.org/state_web_map.htm) lets you find your state's regulator, where you can check complaint histories for insurers, review your rights during a claim, and understand what your policy's language actually requires of both sides.
