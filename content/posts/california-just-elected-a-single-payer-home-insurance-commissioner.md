California elected a single-payer [home insurance](/home-insurance-jewelry-coverage/) commissioner on November 3, 2026, and if you own a home in the state, the implications go well beyond a change in office. Jane Kim, a former San Francisco supervisor, beat state senator Ben Allen with 53% of the vote on a platform that most political observers considered a long shot just 18 months ago: replacing private home insurers with a government-run disaster insurance program. She takes office January 4, 2027, and while no legislation exists yet, the direction of travel is clear and consequential.

The backdrop that made Kim's win possible is a California home insurance market that has been deteriorating in plain sight. The [California FAIR Plan](/california-fair-plan-rates-jump-298-this-fall/), the state's insurer of last resort, held 696,562 policies as of June 2026, a 157% increase since September 2022. A 29.1% average rate hike on FAIR Plan policies took effect October 15, 2026, just weeks before the election. Major private carriers have been restricting or exiting the state for years, and California homeowners collectively spend roughly $18 billion annually on home insurance, with somewhere between $3 billion and $6 billion of that going specifically toward wildfire coverage. That's the money Kim wants redirected to a state agency.

She's pointing to international precedents, specifically Spain, France, and New Zealand, as evidence that government-managed disaster coverage can work at scale without the profit margin and marketing overhead that private insurers build into their premiums.

## What Kim Is Actually Proposing

The proposal isn't a tweak to the [FAIR Plan](/fair-plan-insurance-explained/). It's a structural replacement of the private market for natural disaster coverage with a state-run, nonprofit entity. The FAIR Plan is currently a residual market, a backstop that wasn't designed to cover 700,000 households as a primary insurer. Kim's model would, in theory, be a first-resort program for disaster perils, with the state collecting premiums and paying claims directly.

The international models she cites are worth understanding. France's CatNat system, run through private insurers but backed by a government reinsurer and priced by statute, keeps disaster coverage attached to standard home policies at a fixed surcharge. New Zealand's Earthquake Commission provides a base layer of residential earthquake coverage funded through a levy on private fire policies. Spain runs a similar pooled model through its Consorcio de Compensación de Seguros. None of these are pure single-payer in the American political sense; they involve hybrid structures where the state manages catastrophe risk but private insurers handle other coverage lines. Whether Kim's proposal would mirror that hybrid or go further isn't yet fully defined, which is itself a risk for homeowners trying to plan.

## The Numbers Behind the Problem

The scale of the market breakdown helps explain why a radical proposal got 53% of the vote.

| Total CA homeowner insurance spend (annual) | ~$18 billion |
| Wildfire coverage portion of that spend | $3–$6 billion |
| Kim's election margin | 53% vs. Ben Allen |

That wildfire coverage number is the core of Kim's financial argument. If a state agency collects $3 to $6 billion annually without paying dividends to shareholders, maintaining national advertising budgets, or funding executive compensation, the theoretical savings could lower premiums or expand coverage. That's the argument. The counterargument, which Republican Minority Leader Roger Niello has already raised, is that the same state agency would also absorb catastrophic losses in bad fire years without the ability to spread risk across national or international portfolios the way a large private insurer can.

Niello's concern about taxpayer exposure is not invented political noise. It's a real actuarial question. California's 2017 and 2018 fire seasons combined for over $30 billion in insured losses. A state fund that takes in $4 billion a year in wildfire premiums and then faces a $20 billion loss year would need a capital backstop. Where that money comes from, bonds, a federal arrangement, reinsurance treaties, is the design question that will determine whether this is viable policy or an aspirational sketch.

## What the Legislative Fight Will Look Like

Kim holds regulatory authority over insurance companies doing business in California, but she cannot unilaterally create a new state insurance program. That requires legislation, a state budget appropriation, and almost certainly a ballot measure, since California's constitution and existing law treat insurance markets in ways that make a wholesale replacement legally complex. The Times of San Diego reported in mid-November 2026 that Kim is already pushing her plan publicly, but she's doing so as commissioner-elect without the tools yet to move legislation herself.

The Democratic-controlled legislature will be her path, or her obstacle. Progressive members may be sympathetic. Moderate Democrats in competitive districts may be wary of associating themselves with a program that could face a rough rollout. And Niello's Republican caucus will frame any state takeover as a liability transfer to taxpayers. Expect the initial legislative session to produce hearings and working groups, not a bill that passes. This is a multi-year effort, not a 2027 implementation.

## What California Homeowners Should Actually Do Right Now

The temptation after an election like this is to wait and see. That's understandable, but it's also a risk if you're currently underinsured or stuck on the FAIR Plan paying post-October rates.

A few concrete things worth doing before Kim takes office:

**Review your FAIR Plan policy terms.** The FAIR Plan covers fire, but it has historically excluded liability, theft, and water damage. Many FAIR Plan policyholders carry a separate "Difference in Conditions" policy to fill those gaps. Know whether yours does.

**Get your dwelling replacement cost estimated properly.** California construction costs have increased sharply since 2020. If your dwelling coverage limit was set three or four years ago, it may not reflect current rebuild costs. This is true whether you're with a private carrier or on the FAIR Plan.

**Don't assume Kim's program will be retroactive or universal.** Even if a state disaster insurance program passes in 2028 or 2029, there will almost certainly be transition rules, eligibility criteria, and coverage definitions that don't perfectly match your current policy. Gaps in transition periods can leave homeowners exposed.

**Talk to an independent insurance agent, not a captive one.** A captive agent represents one company. An independent agent can show you what's available across carriers and help you think through your options as the market continues shifting.

Nothing Kim has proposed becomes law the day she takes office. The private market is still there. Your current policy is still your protection. Consulting with a licensed insurance professional before making any coverage changes is advisable, particularly given how fast the California market is moving.

California's home insurance crisis didn't appear overnight, and it won't be solved in a single commissioner's term. But Kim's election signals that the political appetite for a fundamental restructuring is real and growing. Whether the idea works as policy is a genuinely open question. The voters decided they want someone willing to try.
