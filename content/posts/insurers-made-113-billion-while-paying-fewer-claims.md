A bombshell report dropped on November 19, 2026, and if you're a homeowner paying ever-higher insurance premiums, you should read it carefully. Americans for Financial Reform, the Revolving Door Project, and Public Citizen released a joint analysis showing that U.S. home [insurers collected](/insurers-collected-59-more-in-premiums-but-paid-out-far-less/) an estimated $113 billion in excess underwriting profit between 2021 and 2025, even as they closed nearly one in three claims without paying a cent. The industry has spent years telling regulators, legislators, and policyholders that rate increases are unavoidable because climate-driven losses are eating into margins. This report makes that argument a lot harder to swallow.

The numbers are stark. Nationwide premium revenue jumped 59%, from $113.9 billion to $181.2 billion, between 2021 and 2025. Claims payouts rose only 28% over the same period. Premiums grew more than twice as fast as the losses insurers were supposedly trying to cover. In 2025, insurers paid out just 55 cents in claims for every dollar they collected in premiums, compared to a historical range of roughly 69 to 74 cents. In the first half of 2026, that figure dropped further, to 48 cents on the dollar. Texas tells the story in miniature: premiums rose 78% from 2021 to 2025, while claims payouts in that state actually fell 23%.

This isn't a case of one bad actor. This is an industry-wide pattern, and it has real consequences for people who thought they were buying protection.

## The Claim Denial Picture Is Worse Than You Think

The statistic that should stop every homeowner cold: approximately 30% of homeowners claims were closed without payment from 2021 through 2025. That's not 30% of frivolous or fraudulent claims. That's 30% of [all claims](/why-the-biggest-home-insurers-are-denying-nearly-half-of-all-claims/) filed, including by policyholders who'd been paying premiums for years and believed they had coverage.

On top of that, more than 22% of claims that did get paid took longer than 60 days to settle. After a fire, a flood, or a major wind event, 60-plus days without a check can mean months living in temporary housing, running up debt, or making repairs you can't yet afford. Speed of payment matters enormously in a disaster scenario, and the industry isn't delivering it.

What drives denials? A few common culprits: exclusions buried in policy language, depreciation deductions that shrink payouts dramatically, allegations of pre-existing damage, and increasingly, algorithmic claim review tools that flag and deny without meaningful human oversight. That last one is now getting legislative attention. A New Jersey Assembly committee advanced a bill in November 2026 that would prohibit certain AI-driven denials for homeowners, auto, and flood insurance. It's one of the first state-level direct responses to the growing use of automated denial systems. Whether it passes, and whether other states follow, is worth watching.

## The Premium-to-Payout Gap, by the Numbers

It helps to see the trend laid out plainly.

| Year / Period | Loss Ratio (Cents Paid per $1 Premium) |
|---|---|
| 2021–2024 (historical norm) | 69–74 cents |
| 2025 | 55 cents |
| First half of 2026 | 48 cents |

That's not a rounding error or a one-year anomaly. It's a multi-year compression that has moved steadily in the insurer's favor. When an insurer pays out 48 cents for every dollar collected, they're keeping 52 cents after claims. Factor in operating costs and you still have extraordinary margins, margins that the report from Americans for Financial Reform describes as $113 billion in excess profit over five years.

The industry's standard rebuttal is that investment income is volatile, catastrophic loss years spike without warning, and reserves have to be maintained for future claims. Those arguments aren't entirely wrong. But they don't explain a consistent, multi-year widening of the gap between what policyholders pay and what they receive, particularly in a period that included several significant catastrophe years.

## What This Means If You're Renewing Right Now

If your homeowners policy is up for renewal in the next few months, the data above should change how you approach that conversation with your insurer or broker. A few things are worth your attention.

First, check your loss settlement method. Policies that pay actual cash value rather than [replacement cost value](/replacement-cost-value-explained/) will deduct depreciation from every claim. That gap between what your roof cost to build in 2010 and what it costs to replace in 2026 can be enormous. Replacement cost coverage costs more upfront, but it's the only way to avoid a painful shortfall after a major loss.

Second, read your exclusions. Water damage is one of the most common sources of denied claims. Standard homeowners policies typically exclude flood, sewer backup, and gradual water damage. If you're not sure exactly what your water-related exclusions are, call your insurer and ask them to walk you through it in writing.

Third, understand the claims process before you need it. Ask your insurer whether any part of their claims review is automated or AI-assisted, and what the appeal process looks like if a claim is denied. Some insurers will answer directly. If yours won't, that tells you something too.

Finally, the 60-day payment statistic matters when you're shopping. Ask what their average claims settlement time is. It's not a question insurers advertise answers to, but a good independent agent should be able to give you comparative data.

## The Regulatory Gap

State insurance commissioners set rates and can challenge increases. But the approval process is slow, often opaque, and historically weighted toward actuarial data that insurers control. The $113 billion figure in the November 2026 report is a direct challenge to the narrative regulators have largely accepted: that rate hikes are cost-driven and necessary.

New Jersey's AI-denial bill is a narrow but meaningful step. What's missing at a broader level is real-time, state-by-state transparency on claim denial rates, settlement timelines, and loss ratios broken down by company and policy type. Some states publish complaint ratios. Very few require insurers to report denial rates in a standardized, publicly accessible format. That information asymmetry is exactly why reports like this one matter.

If you're not sure whether your current coverage is delivering what you're paying for, a licensed public adjuster or an independent insurance attorney can review your policy before you file a claim, not after. That's the moment when gaps are easiest to address.

The industry's argument has always been that it's on your side when disaster strikes. The November 2026 data suggests the relationship is more complicated than that, and that policyholders who treat their coverage as a guaranteed safety net may be in for a surprise at the worst possible time.
