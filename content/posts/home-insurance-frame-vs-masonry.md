Your construction type is probably the single biggest factor in what you pay for homeowner's insurance, and most buyers don't find that out until after they close. Frame versus masonry isn't just an industry classification, it's a pricing variable that can shift your premium by 5% to 25% depending on your insurer, your state, and what else is on your risk profile.

The short version: frame homes (wood-framed walls) typically cost more to insure than masonry homes (brick or block construction) because wood burns faster and more completely. But the full picture involves fire ratings, wind resistance, and the ISO construction class system that most U.S. insurers use to price policies. Knowing how that system works lets you have a real conversation with your agent instead of just accepting whatever quote lands in your inbox.

## How Insurers Actually Classify Your Home

The Insurance Services Office (ISO) construction classification system runs from class 1 through class 6. Frame is class 1. Masonry construction (brick, block, or stone exterior walls with masonry floors and roof) sits in classes 4 through 6. Classes 2 and 3 cover joisted masonry and noncombustible construction. These aren't just labels, they feed directly into the base rate your insurer charges per $1,000 of [dwelling coverage](/home-insurance-dwelling-coverage/).

Most homeowners have never seen their ISO class. It's not on your [declarations page](/insurance-policy-declarations-page/) in plain language. You may need to ask your agent specifically: "What ISO construction class is my home rated at?" That's a reasonable question and any competent agent can answer it.

The class matters because insurers calibrate their fire loss models around it. A fully masonry home has a substantially lower expected fire severity than a wood-frame home, and that actuarial difference flows downstream into your annual premium.

## The Masonry Veneer Trap

This is where most people get burned (no pun intended). They buy a home with a lovely brick exterior, assume they're getting masonry rates, and then wonder why their premium looks like a frame house. Masonry veneer, a thin layer of brick or stone applied over wood framing, is not full masonry construction. The structural system is still wood. If the brick is cosmetic and the frame is load-bearing, insurers rate it accordingly.

You can usually tell from your home's original building permits or a structural inspection. The question to ask is: "Are the exterior walls structural masonry, or is the brick applied over a wood stud wall?" If it's the latter, expect frame pricing.

## Premium Differences in Practice

Exact figures vary by state, insurer, and property profile, so treat the table below as directional rather than a quote. The ranges reflect general industry patterns; your state insurance department (find yours through the [National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC)](https://www.naic.org/)) can point you to rate filings where specific differences are publicly documented.

| Construction Type | ISO Class | Relative Annual Premium* | Fire Resistance | Wind Resistance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood frame | 1 | Baseline (highest) | Lower | Moderate (flexible) |
| Masonry veneer over frame | 1–2 | Near baseline | Lower-moderate | Moderate |
| Joisted masonry | 2–3 | 5–15% below frame | Moderate | Moderate |
| Brick/block masonry | 4–6 | 10–25% below frame | High | Lower (rigid, cracks) |
| Superior construction (fireproof) | 6 | Lowest | Highest | Variable |

*Premium differences are approximate industry ranges, not guaranteed figures. Actual pricing depends on carrier, location, age of home, and coverage selections.

**Example:** Suppose a wood-frame home in a standard Midwestern market costs $1,800/year to insure for $300,000 in dwelling coverage. A comparable solid brick home on the same street, same age, same coverage amount, might price at $1,500–$1,620, a savings of $180–$300 per year. Over a 30-year mortgage that's $5,400–$9,000, assuming rates stay flat (they won't, but the proportional difference tends to hold). These numbers assume no other risk differences and no discounts applied.

## Wind, Hail, and the Hurricane Exception

The fire story is clean. Wind is messier. In coastal regions, the Gulf Coast, South Florida, the Carolinas, masonry construction's rigidity becomes a liability. Brick and concrete block walls crack under lateral wind loads. Wood-frame construction has more flex, and engineered wood framing with modern hurricane straps and impact windows can outperform a solid block home in wind-driven rain events.

This is why some Florida insurers have historically charged more for block construction in certain high-wind territories, not less. The [NAIC](https://www.naic.org/) publishes consumer guides that touch on regional construction risk factors, and your state's insurance department often has wind mitigation resources specific to local building codes. If you're in a hurricane-prone area, the wind mitigation inspection (typically $150–$200 from a licensed inspector) is worth getting regardless of construction type. It documents your home's features and can directly reduce your wind premium.

## What You Can Actually Do With This Information

If you own a frame home, you're not stuck. A few actions that move the needle:

Installing a monitored fire alarm system and a whole-home sprinkler system (if feasible) can reduce the insurer's fire severity assumption. Home sprinklers are expensive to retrofit but are sometimes required in new construction; if your home already has them, make absolutely sure your insurer knows. Sprinklers can reduce your fire premium significantly depending on the carrier.

If you're buying, ask the seller for the original building permits and a full structural description. Know before you close whether you're buying frame, veneer, or true masonry. It affects your [insurance cost](/how-much-does-homeowners-insurance-cost/) from day one.

If you're shopping policies, request quotes with the construction type explicitly stated, and ask each insurer how they rate masonry veneer specifically. Answers will vary. That variance is information.

Keeping a solid home inventory helps you document contents separately from the dwelling question. Apps like Encircle (encircle.com) or a simple video walkthrough stored in a fireproof document safe can accelerate claims regardless of construction type.
