Complaint ratios are one of the most useful numbers you've never heard of, and your insurer is counting on that. If you're shopping for homeowner coverage or renewing a policy, this single metric can tell you more about day-to-day claims experience than any marketing brochure ever will.

The complaint ratio measures how many justified complaints a company received per 1,000 (or sometimes per million dollars of) premiums written. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners calculates these annually through its Consumer Insurance Information Source, compiling data from every state insurance department. A ratio below 1.0 is generally considered favorable. A ratio of 3.0 or higher should make you pause and ask why.

## What the Ratio Actually Measures

The NAIC's complaint ratio isn't based on every angry customer who fires off an email. It counts only complaints that the state insurance department investigated and found to have merit. That's a meaningful filter. An insurer can have thousands of unhappy customers and a low ratio if most complaints were dismissed, or a small book of business and a high ratio if a handful of valid grievances concentrated in claims denials.

The most common complaint categories for homeowners policies are claim delays, unsatisfactory settlements, and denial of claims. Those three categories account for the overwhelming majority of justified complaints. Marketing disputes and billing issues show up too, but they're rarely the red flags worth tracking.

One thing the ratio can't tell you: *why* a company ranks where it ranks. A high ratio might reflect genuinely bad claims handling. It might also reflect that the insurer writes a lot of policies in a catastrophe-prone state where claim disputes are structurally more common. That context matters, which is why you shouldn't use the ratio as your only filter.

## How to Actually Find the Data

The NAIC's Consumer Information Source (available at naic.org) is the place to start. It's free, and the interface is straightforward.

1. Go to naic.org and search for "Consumer Information Source" or "CIS."
2. Enter the insurer's legal name. Note that many large carriers operate under multiple subsidiary names, so check the exact entity writing your policy, not just the parent brand.
3. Select "Homeowners" as the line of business.
4. Review the complaint index for the most recent three years. The national median is indexed to 1.00, so a score of 2.3 means that company drew 130% more justified complaints per premium dollar than the median insurer.

Your [state's insurance department](https://www.naic.org/state_web_map.htm) may also publish its own complaint data, which filters for residents in your state only. That version can be more relevant than the national figure if the insurer has a small national footprint but a heavy presence in your state.

## Reading the Numbers

| Complaint Index Range | What It Suggests | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Below 0.50 | Well below average complaints | Worth serious consideration |
| 0.50 to 1.00 | Below average to average | Fine baseline; check claim settlement practices |
| 1.01 to 2.00 | Moderately elevated | Ask questions; look at two-to-three-year trend |
| 2.01 to 4.00 | High relative to peers | Dig into complaint categories before committing |
| Above 4.00 | Significantly elevated | Requires strong justification (price, availability) to proceed |

These aren't hard cutoffs. A company with a 1.8 index that has trended down from 3.2 over three years is a better signal than one that's held at 1.9 and inching up. Trend matters as much as the number itself.

## The Gaps the Ratio Won't Show You

Complaint ratios measure what customers reported and what regulators found valid. They don't capture policyholders who accepted a low settlement because they didn't know they could fight it. They don't capture claim delays that never turned into formal complaints. And they tell you nothing about policy language, which is where many disputes actually start.

If you want a more complete picture, pair the NAIC data with the insurer's AM Best financial strength rating (available at ambest.com) and a scan of their policy exclusions. The [IBHS home fortification guides](https://ibhs.org/) won't help you evaluate an insurer directly, but they give you a clear framework for what damage scenarios you need covered, so you know what questions to ask before you sign.

A few specific things worth asking your agent or insurer, in writing:

- What percentage of homeowner claims were closed with payment in the most recent year reported?
- What is the average days-to-close for a claim?
- Does the policy pay replacement cost or actual cash value for personal property?

If an agent can't or won't get you those numbers, that's information too.

**Example:** Suppose you're comparing two insurers, both offering similar premiums on a policy in a coastal state. Insurer A has a complaint index of 0.78 over the past three years, holding steady. Insurer B has an index of 3.1, though it has dropped from 4.6 two years ago. Even with Insurer B's improvement, that historical pattern of elevated complaints in claim-related categories suggests structural issues that may not be fully resolved. If pricing is similar, Insurer A is the more defensible choice absent other information. If Insurer B is significantly cheaper, that price gap needs to be weighed against the realistic probability of a contested claim.

## Sources

- **NAIC Consumer Information Source**: Free insurer complaint ratio database at naic.org, searchable by company and line of business
- **NAIC Annual Complaint Trend Reports**: Year-over-year national complaint data by insurance line, published annually
- **Your state insurance department**: Complaint data filtered to your state; find your department's link at naic.org/state_web_map.htm
- **AM Best**: Financial strength ratings for insurers, available at ambest.com; a measure of claims-paying ability distinct from complaint history
- **Insurance Information Institute (iii.org)**: Plain-language explanations of homeowners coverage types and what dispute resolution options exist
