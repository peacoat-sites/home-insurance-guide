Most homeowners assume they can recall what they own. They can't. After a fire, flood, or theft, memory collapses fast, and that's when insurers ask for receipts, model numbers, and purchase dates on items you bought years ago. A well-built home inventory is what separates a fully paid claim from a lowballed one.

The good news: this doesn't require a lot of time upfront. A single afternoon, done properly, protects you better than years of paying premiums without it. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) has a free tool called the **Home Inventory Checklist** that walks room by room. It's a reasonable starting point, but it doesn't tell you the mistakes people make storing the final document or which categories adjusters scrutinize most.

## Why Standard Policies Make This Harder

Here's something worth understanding before you even pick up a camera. Most homeowner policies cover personal property at either actual cash value (ACV) or [replacement cost value](/replacement-cost-value-explained/) (RCV). ACV pays you what your five-year-old TV is worth today. RCV pays what it costs to buy a comparable one now. The difference on a single electronics claim can be hundreds of dollars. On a whole-house loss, it can be tens of thousands.

The Insurance Information Institute (III) is clear on this point: insurers bear the burden of calculating a loss, but policyholders bear the burden of proving what they owned. No inventory means you're negotiating from memory against a trained adjuster with a depreciation schedule in front of them. That's not a fair fight.

## What to Document, Specifically

Room by room is the right framework. Work systematically so nothing gets skipped. For each item that matters, you want:

- Description (brand, model, color, approximate dimensions for furniture)
- Serial number if it has one
- Purchase price and date, even approximate
- A photo or video showing the item and, where possible, the serial number plate

For clothing, an exact item count per category is enough ("14 dress shirts, 8 pairs of dress shoes"). For jewelry, art, instruments, and firearms, get a formal appraisal. These categories get scrutinized most by adjusters, and they're also capped under standard Coverage C [personal property](/scheduled-personal-property-insurance/) limits.

The categories people most commonly underestimate:

| Category | Why It Gets Disputed | What to Document |
|---|---|---|
| Jewelry & watches | Per-item sublimits, often $1,000-$2,500 | Appraisal, photos, receipts |
| Electronics | Fast depreciation under ACV policies | Model number, purchase date |
| Collectibles / art | Hard to value without provenance | Professional appraisal |
| Power tools | Often excluded or limited in detached structures | Itemized list with purchase prices |
| Clothing | Estimates get cut aggressively | Count by category, note designer items |
| Firearms | Often have separate sublimits | Serial number, purchase records |

The sublimit numbers above are illustrative ranges; your policy's actual caps are in the [declarations page](/insurance-policy-declarations-page/) under Coverage C and any scheduled endorsements. Read that page before assuming.

## Actually Building It

Video is faster than a spreadsheet for most people. Walk each room, narrate what you see ("Samsung 65-inch QLED, serial number on the back panel"), and open cabinets and drawers. Twenty minutes per floor. This creates a defensible record immediately.

If you prefer a document, there are good apps: **Encircle** (used by some insurers themselves) and **BluePlum Home Inventory** are both solid. Some are free; some charge a few dollars a year. A simple spreadsheet in Google Sheets or Excel works just as well if you'll actually maintain it.

**Example:** Assume you own $45,000 in personal property. After a kitchen fire, your insurer estimates the loss at $22,000. You have a video inventory and receipts for your appliances and electronics. Your adjuster raises the estimate to $29,000 based on documented items. Without the inventory, the original estimate stands, and the gap is yours to absorb. These figures are hypothetical, but the dynamic is real and common.

One step people skip: document storage and laundry areas. Chest freezers full of food. Expensive cleaning equipment. Spare building materials. These add up and they never come to mind during a stressful claim.

## Storing It Right

This is where a lot of otherwise good inventories fail. If your only copy is on a laptop that burns in the same fire, it's gone. Options:

- Google Drive, iCloud, or Dropbox (free tiers are fine)
- Email the file to yourself and leave it in the cloud
- A fireproof document safe at home *plus* a cloud backup (belt and suspenders)

A fireproof document safe rated for at least 1 hour at 1700°F is worth considering for original receipts and appraisals. ## When to Update

Once a year works for most people. Tie it to something you already do: your policy renewal date, a tax prep session, or the new year. Any time you spend more than a few hundred dollars on a single item, photograph it and add it to the file before you put it away. That two-minute habit prevents the "I know I had it but I can't prove it" situation.
