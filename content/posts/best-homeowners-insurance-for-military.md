Military families don't shop for homeowners insurance the same way everyone else does. They can't. A deployment order can drop in six weeks before a PCS move, a home might sit vacant for a year while both spouses are overseas, and coverage needs to flex across state lines, overseas assignments, and sudden lease-or-sell decisions that civilians rarely face. Standard policies weren't designed for any of that, and the gaps show up fast when a claim gets filed.

The good news is that the military community has genuine options that most general-audience insurance guides never bother to explain. Some of these are specialized insurers with deep roots in serving servicemembers. Others are conventional companies with riders and flexibility worth knowing about. And a handful are VA-adjacent programs that get confused with actual home insurance but aren't. Knowing the difference matters.

The single most important thing to understand before shopping: "[military discount](/home-insurance-military-discount/)" and "military-focused insurer" are not the same thing. A 5% discount from a standard carrier doesn't address a vacant-home exclusion, a war/government action exclusion, or a total-loss situation where you're deployed and can't be reached.

## What Makes Military Coverage Actually Different

The HO-3 form, which is the standard owner-occupied homeowners policy sold across the U.S., is built around a simple assumption: you live there. Continuously. When that assumption breaks down, so does your coverage.

Vacancy clauses are the biggest landmine. Most HO-3 policies define a home as "vacant" after 30 to 60 consecutive days with no one living in it and reduce or eliminate coverage for specific perils (vandalism, water damage, glass breakage) during that vacancy. Some policies suspend all coverage. If you deploy and your home sits empty, you are potentially uninsured for some of the most common loss types even though you're paying your premium every month.

Deployment also creates a claims-handling problem that few agents discuss. If a pipe bursts at your home in Virginia while you're in Bahrain on a 12-hour shift rotation with limited phone access, who files the claim? Who meets the adjuster? Standard policies typically require the named insured to participate in the [claims process](/homeowners-insurance-claim-process/). Military-focused insurers build in power-of-attorney friendly procedures and designated-contact options because they've dealt with this for decades.

PCS moves create a second [coverage gap](/home-insurance-coverage-gap/): the gap between when your household goods are picked up by the government carrier and when they arrive at your new home. Your homeowners policy's off-premises personal property coverage (usually 10% of your personal property limit) may apply, but it won't cover a full loss of household goods in transit, and it won't cover items in a privately-contracted storage unit the same way it covers items in your home. That's why understanding what your policy says about property "in transit" and "in storage" is not optional reading for military families.

## USAA, AAFMF, and the Military-First Insurers

USAA is the most well-known military-focused financial services company in the country, and for homeowners insurance specifically, it earns its reputation. Its eligibility rules are strict: active duty, Guard and Reserve, veterans with honorable discharges, and their immediate family members (spouses, children, and widowed spouses who haven't remarried). If you qualify, it's one of the first places to get a quote, not because it's always cheapest, but because its policy language is built for military realities. Vacancy coverage during deployments, storage of household goods coverage, and reasonable claims handling procedures when the named insured is unreachable are features that matter.

USAA's homeowners product is a fully realized HO-3 equivalent (they call it their Homeowners Insurance, not a branded form name) with optional upgrades including higher liability limits, earthquake coverage, and flood referrals through the National Flood Insurance Program. It does not automatically cover floods or earthquakes. Those are still separate products. Don't let the USAA brand convince you otherwise.

Armed Forces Benefit Association (AFBA) and the Armed Forces Insurance Exchange (AFI) are smaller players that also specifically serve military members. AFI in particular has long served Fort Leavenworth and surrounding areas and may be worth comparing if you're stationed in the Midwest. These aren't household names, but they're legitimate and regulated.

One thing worth knowing: USAA's homeowners rates are not automatically the lowest for every profile and location. In high-cost states for homeowners insurance (Florida, Louisiana, California, Texas), rates from any insurer can be significant. If you qualify for USAA, compare it against at least one or two other quotes, including military-friendly conventional carriers. Use our [home insurance comparison tool](/best-home-insurance-companies/) to see how top insurers stack up on price, coverage, and claims service.

## Standard Carriers: What "Military Discount" Usually Means

Several large conventional insurers advertise military discounts. These are typically modest percentage reductions off the base premium. What they usually don't include: modified vacancy rules, deployment endorsements, or any special claims handling procedures for deployed servicemembers. You're getting a discount on a standard product, not a modified one.

That said, conventional carriers aren't always the wrong answer. If you own a home in a state where USAA isn't the most competitive on price, or you have a complex property (a farm, a rental unit on the property, a historic home), a conventional carrier with a knowledgeable local agent may serve you better. The key questions to ask any carrier are below.

A note on GEICO: GEICO does not underwrite homeowners insurance itself. It acts as a broker for other companies. The "GEICO homeowners insurance" you see advertised is placed through a partner carrier. Know who that carrier actually is before you buy, because that's who handles your claim.

## The SCRA Question Everyone Gets Wrong

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA) is real and meaningful legislation. It caps interest rates on pre-service financial obligations at 6%, provides protections against eviction, and allows lease termination without penalty in certain circumstances. What it does not do is provide homeowners insurance coverage, modify an insurance policy, or protect a deployed servicemember from vacancy exclusions.

This confusion causes real problems. Some servicemembers assume SCRA protects them from insurance coverage lapses during deployment. It doesn't. The SCRA interacts with certain insurance contexts (notably state-mandated cancellation rules and some premium provisions), but those interactions are narrow and don't substitute for a policy that actually covers your home while you're gone.

If you're uncertain about your SCRA rights, the DoD's Office of Legal Policy maintains guidance on the act, and your installation's Judge Advocate General (JAG) office offers free legal assistance that includes insurance questions.

## Coverage Benchmarks for Military-Owned Homes

| Coverage Type | What to Require | What to Avoid | Why It Matters for Military |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dwelling coverage | Replacement cost value (RCV) | Actual cash value (ACV) | ACV deducts depreciation; RCV rebuilds at today's costs |
| Personal property | Replacement cost or scheduled items | ACV only | Household goods depreciate fast; ACV pays pennies on a 5-year-old TV |
| Vacancy provision | Endorsement extending vacancy to 60-180 days | Standard 30-day vacancy clause | Deployments and unaccompanied tours last longer than 30 days |
| Loss of use / Additional living expense | At least 20% of dwelling value | Less than 10% | PCS disruptions can extend temporary housing situations |
| Liability | $300,000 minimum; umbrella available | $100,000 | High-traffic households, frequent moves create exposure |
| Flood coverage | Separate NFIP policy or private flood | Assuming HO-3 covers it | No standard homeowners policy covers flood |
| Personal property in storage | Confirm off-premises % applies to storage | Blanket exclusion for storage units | PCS moves often require extended storage |

If you're not sure what your home would cost to rebuild from the ground up today, that number is what your dwelling coverage should be based on. Use this [home replacement cost calculator](/home-replacement-cost-calculator/) to get an estimate before you finalize a policy limit.

Replacement cost estimation matters especially for military families because the home may have been purchased years ago at a price that no longer reflects current construction costs. Insuring to 80-90% of a purchase price from several years ago is a coverage gap waiting to open.

## Renting Out Your Home During PCS Orders

This is one of the most common situations military families face and one of the most commonly mishandled insurance situations. If you receive PCS orders and decide to rent your home rather than sell it, your existing homeowners policy almost certainly needs to change. Standard HO-3 policies cover owner-occupied dwellings. The moment a tenant moves in, your occupancy status changes and your coverage may be void.

The product you need is a landlord policy, also called a dwelling fire policy (DP-3 is the standard form). It covers the structure, your personal property that remains on the premises (appliances you leave for tenants, for example), and liability. It does not cover the tenant's belongings. Tenants need their own renters insurance.

USAA does offer a landlord policy product. If you're renting through a military-focused carrier, ask specifically for their rental dwelling or landlord product, not the homeowners product you already have.

The Insurance Information Institute (III) has a solid overview of landlord insurance and what's typically covered under a DP-3 form at iii.org, which is worth reading before you decide whether to convert a policy or add a rider.

## Questions to Ask Before You Buy

Don't just compare premiums. Ask every insurer these questions directly, and get the answers in writing or by reference to the specific policy form:

How does the policy define vacancy, and what happens to coverage during a vacancy period? Can I get a vacancy endorsement, and what does it cost?

If I'm deployed and unreachable, who can file a claim on my behalf? Does the policy accommodate a power of attorney?

Does off-premises personal property coverage apply to household goods in government storage during a PCS move?

If I convert the home to a rental property, what are my coverage obligations? Will my current policy void, and what's the transition process?

Does the policy have a war exclusion, and how does it define "war"? (Some policies use broad language that could theoretically touch military action, though in practice most carriers clarify this doesn't affect servicemembers' own property claims. Still worth confirming.)

The IBHS (Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety) publishes home fortification and construction resilience guides at ibhs.org that are useful for anyone evaluating a home's risk profile before insuring it, particularly in storm-prone areas where military installations are frequently located (coastal Virginia, South Carolina, Hawaii, Texas Gulf Coast).
