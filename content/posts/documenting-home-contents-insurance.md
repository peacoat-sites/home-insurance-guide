Most people who file a homeowner's claim discover the same painful truth at the worst possible moment: their insurer will pay for what they can *prove* they owned, not what they remember owning. A home inventory is how you do that proving. Without one, you're negotiating against an adjuster who does this every day, armed with nothing but your memory and a few blurry photos from a birthday party two years ago.

The good news is that building a solid record isn't complicated. It just requires doing it before you need it.

## Why Your Current Documentation Probably Isn't Enough

A lot of homeowners think they're covered because they once emailed themselves some photos or filled out a spreadsheet that now lives on a laptop inside the house. Neither solves the problem. The Insurance Information Institute (III) has long noted that only a minority of homeowners maintain a current, complete home inventory, which is part of why disputed and underpaid claims are so common.

What adjusters actually want to see: receipts, model numbers, serial numbers, purchase dates, and ideally a visual record. They're not trying to be difficult. They have a claims process that requires documented proof before releasing funds. The better your documentation matches that process, the faster and more completely you get paid.

There's also a subtler issue. Standard homeowner's policies have sub-limits for certain categories of property regardless of your overall [personal property coverage](/homeowners-insurance-personal-property-coverage/) limit. Electronics, jewelry, firearms, and silverware each have their own caps in many policies, and those caps can be surprisingly low. No amount of documentation will get you paid above those sub-limits without a scheduled endorsement. So documentation and coverage review need to happen together.

## How to Build a Home Inventory That Actually Works

The method matters less than the completeness. Use whatever you'll actually finish.

**Video walkthrough.** Walk through every room narrating as you go. Open drawers. Open closets. Read model numbers off appliances and electronics. This takes about 45 minutes in a typical house and produces more usable evidence than a spreadsheet that's missing half the items. Upload immediately to cloud storage (Google Drive, iCloud, Dropbox). Done.

**Dedicated home inventory apps.** Apps like Encircle let you log items with photos, serial numbers, and purchase prices. The structured format is easier for adjusters to work with than raw video alone. For anyone with a lot of high-value items, the app is worth the extra time.

**Spreadsheet backup.** Even if you use an app or video, export a CSV or PDF copy. Store it somewhere that isn't your house: a safety deposit box, a trusted family member's home, or cloud storage. [A fireproof document safe](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=fireproof+document+safe&tag=contentportfo-20) can protect physical copies of receipts and policy documents, though it's not a substitute for off-site digital backup. *(This site may earn a commission on purchases made through links.)*

**What to capture for each item:**
- Description, brand, model, and serial number
- Purchase price and approximate purchase date (a bank or credit card statement works if you don't have the receipt)
- Current replacement cost (search the item on a retailer's website and screenshot the price)
- Photo of the item and, separately, a photo of any serial number or identifying label

You don't need to do every item in one sitting. Start with the highest-value rooms: kitchen appliances, home office, bedroom jewelry and electronics. Get those done in one session and you've captured the bulk of your exposure.

## Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value: The Number That Changes Everything

This isn't just an academic distinction. It directly determines how much you receive for a claim.

| Policy Type | How Loss Is Paid | Example: 5-Year-Old Laptop (Originally $1,200) | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actual Cash Value (ACV) | Depreciated value at time of loss | ~$300-$500 (varies by depreciation schedule) | Lower-premium buyers accepting higher risk |
| Replacement Cost Value (RCV) | Cost to buy equivalent new item today | ~$1,100-$1,400 (current market price) | Most homeowners with significant contents |
| Extended RCV | RCV plus a buffer (often 20-25%) for price increases | ~$1,300-$1,750 | Households with newer, higher-value contents |
| Scheduled Personal Property | Agreed or appraised value, item by item | Appraised amount, no depreciation | High-value items: jewelry, art, instruments |

The depreciation on a 5-year-old laptop isn't hypothetical softening. Adjusters use published depreciation tables, and consumer electronics depreciate fast. Without RCV coverage, you might receive a fraction of what you'd need to replace it. Check your [declarations page](/insurance-policy-declarations-page/) right now: look for "Loss Settlement" and confirm it says "Replacement Cost" rather than "Actual Cash Value."

**Example:** Assume a home office contains a laptop ($1,400 today), a monitor ($500), a printer ($300), a desk chair ($600), and miscellaneous peripherals ($400). Total replacement cost: $3,200. Under an ACV policy with an average 50% depreciation on 4-year-old items, the payout might be roughly $1,600. Under an RCV policy, the same claim pays close to $3,200 minus the deductible. That gap, multiplied across a full house, is why coverage type matters far more than most buyers realize when shopping on premium alone.

## Scheduled Endorsements: When a List Isn't Enough

Standard personal property coverage has sub-limits. A common structure is something like $1,500 for jewelry or $2,500 for firearms per occurrence, regardless of your total coverage amount. If you own a $5,000 engagement ring and have only standard coverage, $1,500 is your ceiling on that ring no matter how good your documentation is.

A scheduled personal property endorsement (sometimes called a "floater" or "rider") covers specific items at their appraised or agreed value. For jewelry, art, musical instruments, cameras, and collectibles, this is frequently the only way to be fully covered. Appraisals are typically required, and you'll pay a small additional premium per item.

The process: get the item appraised by a qualified appraiser, provide the appraisal to your insurer, and ask them to add it as a scheduled item. The American Society of Jewelry Appraisers (americansocietyofjewelryappraisers.com) has an appraiser locator if you need a starting point for jewelry. The IBHS (Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety, at ibhs.org) publishes guidance on protecting and documenting personal property that's worth reading alongside your policy documents.

Revisit your scheduled items every few years. Values change, and an appraisal from a decade ago may significantly understate current replacement cost.

## Storing and Maintaining Your Inventory

The most common failure isn't making the inventory. It's letting it go stale. A record of everything you owned three years ago is better than nothing, but it's going to miss the television you bought last year, the KitchenAid mixer, and the new laptop.

A practical cadence: do a full walkthrough once a year (tie it to a renewal date so you don't forget), and add major purchases within a week of buying them. Keep a photo and receipt in a designated folder in cloud storage. [A water leak sensor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=water+leak+sensor&tag=contentportfo-20) near appliances won't document your contents, but it can prevent the claim from happening in the first place. *(This site may earn a commission on purchases made through links.)*

Your insurer should ideally never see your home inventory. When they do, you want it to be unambiguous.
